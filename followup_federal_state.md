# Pausing, deferring, forgiving: who diesel tax relief actually helps

Everyone is talking about diesel taxes. Or, rather, talking about getting rid of diesel taxes. Well, not getting rid of them permanently, but pausing them. Well, pausing state taxes, but deferring federal taxes. Deferring in hopes of forgiving, which is a lot like pausing. Anyhow, it's not important.

Dyed diesel is the red, off-road fuel farmers already put in tractors and combines. It's sold without the 24.4-cent federal diesel tax, but that tax comes due, along with steep penalties, if the fuel ends up in a truck on a public road. On Oct. 5, President Trump [ordered](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/eo-14435.pdf) the Treasury to defer that tax for dyed diesel used on the road through Dec. 31. The deferred tax isn't due until a date the Treasury has yet to set, and the order asks Treasury to "explore avenues, including legislation," to forgive it. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-provides-dyed-diesel-fuel-relief-related-to-excise-tax-following-president-trumps-executive-order) has since said it won't penalize anyone for running dyed diesel on the highway. Clear diesel is taxed as before.

States have moved too. Seventeen have suspended their diesel tax or let some drivers run dyed diesel on the road (Figure 1). Ohio and Georgia suspended the tax outright. Missouri, Texas and Mississippi opened dyed diesel to all drivers. Twelve others, including [Michigan](https://www.michiganfarmnews.com/about/news-media/gov-whitmer-signs-waiver-granting-dyed-diesel-tax-relief-remainder-2026), limited it to farm and forestry vehicles. Most of it ends by Dec. 31 (Figure 2).

In a [recently published piece]([NDSU LINK]), I found that North Dakota's dyed-diesel order would save a typical 2,000-acre corn-and-soybean farm $170 this fall. That's not nothing, but it is pretty close to nothing.

My argument is this: state relief is harmless but won't do much. Federal relief helps the drivers who can switch, but it puts some risk on the people who already buy most of their diesel dyed, and that is mostly farmers.

## State relief: small and harmless

Farmers burn most of their diesel in the field, where it's already dyed and untaxed. Only hauling fuel is taxed, and North Dakota's order cut that by just 19 cents. Gabriel Lade at Ohio State [found the same]([LADE LINK]) for Ohio's broader 47-cent holiday: about $195 for a 1,000-acre farm. "Most of our diesel use is behind us," North Dakota Farmers Union president Matt Perdue told the [AP]([AP LINK]).

It's harmless because one state can't move the price of diesel. Clear and dyed diesel are the same fuel at the same wholesale price, and when one state cuts its tax, suppliers can pull fuel from its neighbors. Lade doesn't expect Ohio's holiday to move wholesale prices at all.

## Federal relief: a different animal

A national cut has no neighbors to pull from. In a [second post]([LADE LINK 2]), Lade argues that with refineries near capacity, a national cut raises the wholesale price of diesel. Dyed diesel buyers then pay more without getting any tax break. His 1,000-acre Ohio farm buys 75% of its diesel dyed, so it comes out behind if more than about 25% of the tax cut shows up in the wholesale price. His central estimate is 31%.

The actual order is narrower than a full federal holiday, because most drivers won't or can't switch. In about 33 states, running dyed diesel on the road is still illegal under state law, and 12 more allow it only for farm trucks. Some states still collect their own tax on it. The federal tax is only deferred, so it could come due later. Many dyed-diesel pumps can't fit a semi. "The largest diesel users are the least likely to use dyed diesel," GasBuddy's Patrick De Haan told the AP. Less switching means less pressure on the national price.

But the switching that does happen lands on the rural dyed-diesel supply that farmers depend on at harvest. Michigan State's David Ortega told the AP there is "a real chance this could backfire and make the situation worse for farmers." Perdue expects "a rush for dyed diesel."

The result:

- **Winners:** drivers who switch, mostly farmers trucking their own grain and local haulers where it's legal. A North Dakota farmer who switches truck fuel now avoids 43.4 cents a gallon instead of 19. If the deferral is forgiven, my $170 estimate becomes about $390.
- **Mostly unaffected:** most truckers, who keep buying clear diesel.
- **At risk:** heavy dyed-diesel users who can't switch, including farm field work, construction and heating. They get no tax break and absorb any rise in the price of dyed diesel.

## Bottom line

State diesel relief is cheap, popular and harmless, and it's also tiny. Federal relief helps the drivers it reaches. But farmers buy most of their diesel dyed, so a policy sold as help for farmers puts much of its risk on them. That risk grows the longer the deferral runs and the more states pile on. Watch the gap between dyed and clear diesel prices: if it narrows, farmers are paying for someone else's tax break.
