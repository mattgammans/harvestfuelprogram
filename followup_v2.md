Everyone is talking about diesel taxes. Or, rather, talking about getting rid of diesel taxes. Well, not getting rid of them permanently, but pausing them. Well, pausing state taxes, but deferring federal taxes. Deferring in hopes of forgiving, which is a lot like pausing. Anyhow, it's really not important.

President Trump has [ordered](https://www.whitehouse.gov/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/eo-14435.pdf) the Treasury to defer the federal tax on red-dyed diesel used on public roads from Oct. 5 through Dec. 31. Dyed diesel is the off-road fuel farmers put in tractors and combines. It's sold without the 24.4-cent federal diesel tax, but that tax, plus steep penalties, comes due if the fuel ends up in a truck on the highway. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-provides-dyed-diesel-fuel-relief-related-to-excise-tax-following-president-trumps-executive-order) has already waived the penalties. The deferral itself still awaits Treasury guidance, and once granted, the tax won't need to be paid until a date Treasury has yet to set. The order also directs Treasury to explore ways, including legislation, to make the deferral permanent forgiveness. Clear diesel at the pump is taxed as before.

Several economists have raised the possibility that the federal deferral may be bad for some diesel users. The logic is that, by expanding the set of legal uses of red-dyed diesel, the demand for diesel generally will increase, which will raise prices. Clear and dyed diesel are the same fuel and share a wholesale price, so if highway drivers switch, farmers pay more for the dyed fuel they burn in the field without getting any tax break. Gabriel Lade at Ohio State [works through the math]([LADE LINK]): a 1,000-acre Ohio corn-and-soybean farm buys about 75% of its diesel dyed, so it comes out behind if more than about 25% of a national tax cut shows up as a higher wholesale price. With refineries running near capacity, his central estimate is 31%. Michigan State's David Ortega told the [AP]([AP LINK]) there is "a real chance this could backfire and make the situation worse for farmers."

How big that effect gets depends on how many drivers actually switch, and the barriers are real. In about 33 states, running dyed diesel on the road is still illegal under state law, and 12 more allow it only for farm trucks. The federal tax is deferred, not forgiven, so anyone who switches could owe it later. Many dyed-diesel pumps can't fit a semi. "The largest diesel users are the least likely to use dyed diesel," GasBuddy's Patrick De Haan told the AP. So the national price effect is probably smaller than in Lade's scenario, but whatever switching does happen draws on the same rural dyed-diesel supply farmers rely on at harvest.

Many states have adopted similar measures. North Dakota let licensed agricultural vehicles run dyed diesel on highways from Sept. 29 through Nov. 30; dyed diesel there carries 4 cents of state tax, versus 23 cents on clear. Texas lifted its dyed-diesel restrictions for all drivers on Sept. 28, though the [state Comptroller](https://comptroller.texas.gov/taxes/fuels/diesel.php) says the 20-cent state tax is still owed, and the proclamation lapses after 30 days unless renewed. [Michigan](https://www.michiganfarmnews.com/about/news-media/gov-whitmer-signs-waiver-granting-dyed-diesel-tax-relief-remainder-2026), on Oct. 12, let vehicles with farm or wood-harvester plates use dyed diesel on public roads through Dec. 31, skipping a state tax of about 52 cents. Should we have similar concerns that state-level relief will hurt farmers? I don't think so. No individual state uses enough diesel for a tax cut of 20 to 50 cents to meaningfully move national demand. When one state cuts its tax, suppliers can pull fuel from its neighbors, a point Lade makes about Ohio's 47-cent holiday. Most state orders are also limited to farm and forestry trucks, a sliver of the market. These state-level policies should be thought of as small potatoes, or in more modern parlance, a nothingburger: harmless and pointless. In a [recently published piece]([NDSU LINK]), I analyzed the impact of North Dakota's dyed-diesel order and found that it would save a typical 2,000-acre corn-and-soybean farm $170 this fall. Not nothing, but kind of close to nothing next to the roughly $22,000 that farm's harvest diesel bill has gone up. Lade found much the same for Ohio: about $195 for a 1,000-acre farm. As North Dakota Farmers Union president Matt Perdue told the AP, "Most of our diesel use is behind us."

Diesel relief is fairly cheap, popular, and mostly harmless. It's also tiny. Federal relief helps the drivers it affects, with some costs to those who already benefit from tax-free diesel. State policies are less likely to drive up prices, but do little to lower farmers' costs this fall.
